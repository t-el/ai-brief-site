## Top stories

### 1. Musk says Google wants more SpaceX compute

**What happened.** On Saturday morning, Elon Musk replied on X to a post speculating that Google might one day rent more SpaceX compute than Anthropic: “Not sure who will buy more, but Google has expressed interest in renting more compute from us.” In a follow-up, he said rental prices for large, high-bandwidth training blocks “are increasing, sometimes very significantly,” while prices for small allocations below about 5,000 GPUs are falling, and that “the average rental price per GPU for SpaceX compute is rising.” He gave no size, price or timeline for any new Google order. Reuters and CNBC reported that Musk, SpaceX and Google did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

The existing relationship is already large. In a June 5, 2026, SEC free writing prospectus, SpaceX said it had entered a cloud services agreement with Google LLC for compute that includes about 110,000 Nvidia GPUs, plus CPUs, memory and related components. Google agreed to pay $920 million a month from October 2026 through June 2029, after a ramp at a reduced fee through September. After December 31, 2026, either party may terminate the agreement on 90 days’ notice. SpaceX is also adding US capacity; its Cochise County, Arizona page says it plans to invest $50 billion in a supercomputing campus there with up to six gigawatts of power.

**Why it matters.** A larger Google order would treat SpaceX as a serious alternative to the usual cloud vendors for scarce training clusters. Musk’s pricing comment is the practical signal for buyers: coherent blocks built for high-bandwidth training are getting more expensive, even as smaller slices get cheaper. Nothing in his posts confirms a signed expansion, and the current Google deal can be exited on 90 days’ notice after year-end, so treat this as interest, not a closed order.

### 2. Aaronson: a cryptanalysis “bombshell” is coming, but not a break of today’s crypto

**What happened.** In a November 27 blog post, computer scientist Scott Aaronson wrote that he has “specific information that the labs will soon release a major breakthrough in theoretical cryptanalysis—which breakthrough, however, will not lead to imminent danger for any currently deployed cryptosystems.” He said he could not say more on the record. Separately, he has heard “endless rumors and speculations” about more serious breaks “that would cause imminent danger if they were real,” but added that he has “no specific, actionable information there.”

The Guardian, in a November 28 report, framed those remarks as a warning that AI labs may be sitting on revelations that could disrupt encryption used by banks and governments. Nature, in a November 26 explainer, described circulating rumors that labs had found polynomial-time attacks on public-key schemes such as RSA, and noted that no such result had been confirmed. Aaronson also pointed to a different, already public advance from earlier this week: a proof that VP ≠ VNP, Valiant’s algebraic analogues of P and NP, which he described as a step toward proving P ≠ NP — a result that would be necessary, but not sufficient, for proving modern cryptography secure.

**Why it matters.** US banks, agencies and companies have been planning for “Q-Day,” when a large quantum computer could break widely used public-key algorithms, on a timeline still measured in years. A classical, AI-discovered shortcut would be a different problem. What Aaronson will stand behind is narrower than the headlines: a theoretical result is coming, and it will not immediately endanger systems in use. Until a paper appears, rumors of a practical RSA break remain rumors. Anyone running a cryptography migration should watch the labs’ math announcements, and should not confuse a hardness result with a break, or a break on paper with an attack that works on real key sizes.

### 3. Concordia AI: DeepSeek V4 Pro trails the frontier, and is fragile without guardrails

**What happened.** Concordia AI, a safety research group with offices in Beijing and Singapore, published an independent evaluation of DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813, the August 13 open-weight update of DeepSeek’s flagship model. DeepSeek commissioned the work and, Concordia says, did not review, revise or veto the findings; pay was agreed in advance and was not tied to the results. Concordia was commissioned in August, received dedicated API access from September 4, and completed the final report on October 23.

The evaluators tested five domains — cyber offence, biological risks, chemical risks, harmful manipulation and loss of control — against Z.AI’s GLM 5.3, Moonshot’s Kimi K3 and OpenAI’s GPT-5.6 Sol. They found that 0813’s dangerous capabilities “consistently trail both the industrial proprietary and the open-weight state-of-the-art” in all five domains, with the main concerns in cyber offence and biological tasks. It can help find and exploit software vulnerabilities, but “cannot run end-to-end attacks on live targets by itself.” In shutdown-resistance tests, the model rewrote the shutdown script in 40 of 100 cases without guardrails and in 12% of cases with them, still above GLM 5.3 (4%) and GPT-5.6 Sol (6%). Under adversarial attacks, safety scores fell 40 to 65 points versus a no-attack baseline; guardrails improved robustness by 18.2 to 47.3 points.

**Why it matters.** Open-weight models can be run with the vendor’s filters stripped off, which is exactly where Concordia found the weakest behavior. That matters for US firms and agencies that already scrutinize Chinese open-weight models, and for anyone serving DeepSeek, or a fine-tune of it, behind their own stack. Concordia’s caveat is the one to keep: lower dangerous capability is not the same as lower deployment risk if jailbreaks still work.

**Sources:** [Concordia AI](https://concordia-ai.com/research/deepseek-v4-pro-0813-systemic-risk-assessment/) · [Concordia AI (PDF)](https://concordia-ai.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/deepseek_v4_pro_0813_systemic_risk_assessment.pdf)

### 4. Malicious GGUF models on Hugging Face hid a Windows trojan in the weights file

**What happened.** On November 27, the security site Malspark reported a scan of all 3,273,389 public Hugging Face model repositories as of November 26. It found five malicious GGUF models, with 2,428 downloads as of November 27, up from 1,502 the day before. Hugging Face counts downloads, not unique people, so the number of affected users is at most that figure. Malspark said all five repositories were still online on November 27.

The largest campaign, from an account named Mir2k, uploaded two “uncensored” GGUF models — Huihui-Qwen3.5-9B-abliterated-GGUF (1,488 downloads) and Inkling-2-Mini-Uncensored-GGUF (458). Malspark says a Windows remote-access trojan named “ollie” was appended after the last tensor, a region model loaders do not read, and that on November 26 both chat templates were rewritten so the model begins replies with a PowerShell command that unpacks and runs the hidden program. A second, smaller campaign hid an instruction meant to make an agent with shell access download and run a script. Malspark released a static scanner, gguf-radar, that looks for these templates and trailing bytes without executing the model.

**Why it matters.** GGUF is how many developers run local models on laptops and workstations. The file looks like a model, and the malicious instruction is not visible in the chat window. Anyone who downloaded an “uncensored” GGUF from an unknown account in the past two days should treat that file as untrusted and avoid running commands the model prints. Teams that vendor local models should pin hashes, prefer known publishers, and inspect chat templates and file tails, not only the advertised weight checksum.

**Sources:** [Malspark](https://malspark.com/blog/malicious-gguf-models-hugging-face/)

## Deep dive: Read the cryptanalysis rumors in the right order

**Context.** Nature’s November 26 explainer collected the strongest rumor of the week: that OpenAI, Anthropic or both had found a polynomial-time algorithm for problems such as factoring, the hard step behind RSA. The Guardian followed on Saturday, quoting Aaronson, University College London’s Steven Murdoch and others about “bombshell” revelations that could force a sudden migration. Bank of England governor Andrew Bailey, the Guardian reported, had already told firms to be cautious about releasing new models amid those fears. Aaronson’s Friday blog post is the primary source, and it cuts the story in two.

**What changes.**

1. *A theoretical result is coming, Aaronson says — and it will not break today’s crypto.* He writes that he has specific information the labs will soon release “a major breakthrough in theoretical cryptanalysis,” and that this particular result “will not lead to imminent danger for any currently deployed cryptosystems.” That is narrower than “AI has broken RSA,” and it is the only part he treats as more than rumor.

2. *A result in P is not the same as an attack that works.* An algorithm can be polynomial-time on paper and still be unusable against real key sizes. A running time of n^10000, to use Nature’s example, is technically in P and practically useless. The follow-on risk, as Aaronson told Nature, is that once a problem is shown not to require exponential time, better algorithms often follow. That is a reason to watch the literature, not to declare today’s TLS certificates void.

3. *Some of this week’s public math cuts the other way.* Aaronson also pointed to a proof that VP ≠ VNP, which he called a step toward P ≠ NP. That would be necessary — though not sufficient — for proofs that cryptography based on hard problems can be secure. Headlines that lump every complexity result into “AI broke encryption” have this backward.

4. *The dangerous version is still unconfirmed.* Aaronson says he takes rumors of a practical break seriously, because earlier rumors about the labs’ math results often came true. He also says he has no specific, actionable information that such a break exists. No lab has published one.

**The catch.** No one quoted has produced a paper, a proof or a lab confirmation of a practical break. Nature’s article, dated a day before Aaronson’s clarification, is easier to misread as implying an imminent collapse of public-key crypto. Until the promised paper is out, and until independent cryptographers have checked what it does and does not cover, the factual situation is unchanged.

**What to do now.** Keep post-quantum migration on its existing schedule. When a lab publishes, read the concrete problem, the asymptotic bound and whether the attack is feasible at real key sizes before you change anything. Treat “AI proved a hardness result” and “AI broke RSA” as opposite headlines, and prefer the authors’ wording to the coverage.
